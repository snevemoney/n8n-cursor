# Bizcomm checklists (clustered)

**Use when:** a task asks what business communication is, who a message is for, what it is trying to do, which channel to send it on, what tone to take, whether anyone actually understood it, how to write a memo/letter/e-mail, how to give a workplace presentation, or how to deliver negative news without burning the relationship.

**Do not use when:** the job is reconstructing or taking a Saylor (or any) exam, dumping Thill/Bovée or any all-rights-reserved textbook, running COMM100 as general speech-comm, treating the prompt as BUS208 POLC or BUS209 three-level OB, writing a dark-PR suppression plan, or coaching a specific live crisis beyond the undergrad checklist.

This is **one** skill. It is not "Unit 1 skill + Unit 2 skill…". BUS210's nine units are one argument: **business communication is a problem-solving process of understanding and sharing meaning**; you design a message by checking **audience, purpose, channel, tone, and feedback** — then you apply that same five-check to writing, presentations, bad news, self-talk, culture, and groups.

## 0. Hygiene

- Label **school**. This is the **standard undergraduate business-communication intro** (Saylor BUS210; rhetorical situation; eight-component process; Saylor *Business Communication for Success* v1.0). Shared with almost every undergrad bizcomm survey. It is not the only communication theory (media studies, conversation analysis, Cialdini persuasion labs). Say so if the user is not inside this course.
- Label **claim vs data**. NACE 2009 skill lists, National Commission on Writing 2004, AMA 2007 e-mail-monitoring %, Atlanta "Coke," Honda Fitta, Intel Pentium are **course-era illustrations**. Re-fetch if the user needs current numbers.
- Bizcomm ≠ COMM100 ≠ BUS208 ≠ BUS209. COMM100 is general human communication. BUS208 asks what the *manager's job* is (POLC). BUS209 asks how *people behave* at three levels. This skill asks how a **message is designed so meaning is actually shared**. You will share names (group vs team, conflict, culture) with those courses; you will not collapse into them.
- No exam items. No all-rights-reserved chapter dumps. CC OER: summarize.

## 1. Procedure — answer a bizcomm question in five checks

Run in order. Skip a check only if the user already constrained the frame. If a check fails, name it; do not "send harder."

Before the five checks, write the **identity** (ch.1):

**Communication** = the process of **understanding and sharing meaning**. Words are not the meaning. Meaning is negotiated (constructivist / common ground). Roles overlap (transactional): you are source and receiver at once.

**Eight components — name which one failed before naming a style rule:**

| Component | Question |
|---|---|
| Source | Who is encoding, and are they prepared and ethical? |
| Message | Primary (intentional) vs secondary (unintentional impression) vs auxiliary (how it is said)? Residual thought after they leave? |
| Channel | How does it travel? See Check C. |
| Receiver | Who is decoding, and what map are they using? See Check A. |
| Feedback | What comes back, including silence? See Check E. |
| Environment | Physical and psychological atmosphere. |
| Context | Which of five: intrapersonal / interpersonal / group / public / mass? What do they expect? |
| Interference / noise | Physical, or psychological (you started the reply before you finished reading). |

Business communication as **problem solving:** situation → possible strategies → best action → design the message → deliver it. The five checks *are* that design.

Two responsibilities that sit under every check: **prepared** (organized, clear, concise, punctual) and **ethical** (egalitarian, respectful, trustworthy; Golden Rule). Prepared-but-unethical is still a fail.

### Check A — Audience (always)

Who is receiving this, and what will they do with it?

**See yourself first (intrapersonal is the first audience).** Attitudes change easily; beliefs less so; values almost not. Self-image (how you describe yourself) vs self-esteem (how you feel about that) → self-concept. Looking-glass self: you are also wearing their view of you. Self-fulfilling prophecy (Pygmalion): you cue an expectation, they match it, it comes true — including "I am bad at presentations."

**Perception is not a camera.** They **select** (exposure / attention / retention), **organize** (Gestalt: they will fill your potholes — so don't leave them), **interpret** (preunderstanding + conventions). You cannot perceive everything; neither can they. Internal stimuli (hunger, anxiety) compete with you.

**Iceberg:** what you can see (size, age, gender, job, education, prior knowledge, time/space allotted) sits on a larger mass of values, scripts, and culture. Demographics are a start, not the person. Two people in the same demographic are still two maps.

Ask, in this order:

1. How big is the audience, and is it one person, a group, a public, or a mass?
2. What do they already know, and what jargon is noise for them?
3. What do they need, want, fear, and have time for? ("What's in it for me" is a legitimate attention statement, not a trick.)
4. What is their probable response? Who is the actual decision-maker who will open it?
5. Fairness / reciprocity: mutuality (common ground), nonjudgmentalism, honesty (including what you are *not* saying), respect. They are silently asking "why should I listen / what does this have to do with me?"

**Active listening / reading (you are an audience too):** eyes on speaker or page; don't interrupt / don't multitask; restated meaning; clarifying questions. Hard conversations: set a time, nonjudgmental, "I" statements, keep the relationship.

Culture (Unit 8 overlay, when borders, high/low context, or "that's rude here" are in play): culture rewrites what counts as a polite channel, how direct the purpose may be, which tone is respectful, and whether feedback is given in public. Acculturation is a process. Do not export U.S. "get to the point" as a law.

If you cannot name the audience, you do not have a message. You have a diary.

### Check B — Purpose (always)

Why are you sending this, and what should be true after they receive it?

**General purpose** (pick one primary): inform, persuade, entertain, facilitate interaction, motivate. Informative is the default workplace job. Persuade is a different job (change in belief or action), not "inform but pushier."

**Specific purpose** = the desired **outcome** (a record that they were told; attendance at a meeting; a signed contract; a corrected shipment). If you cannot finish the sentence "after this, they will ___," you do not have a specific purpose.

Then the rest of the named planning checklist (Thill/Bovée as adapted in BCS ch.5 — use the tool, do not dump Pearson):

- Is the purpose **realistic**?
- Is the **timing** right (holiday mail, 4:45 p.m. Friday, after the newspaper already ran it)?
- Are the **sources** credible, and will this audience grant that credibility?
- Does the message **reflect positively on the business** (tone of "we are here to serve," not "cover our inbox")?
- Is the information **accurate, ethical, and pertinent**? Bait-and-switch, lying by omission, and plagiarism (verbatim, paraphrase, or stolen idea) are ethics fails, not style.

Informative messages cover **who / what / when / where / how / why**. Why is optional when the job is to report facts without interpretation. Do not smuggle a sales pitch into an informative record.

Five-part message (when the payload is more than a ping): **attention** → **introduction** (topic + relationship) → **body** (points, support, transitions) → **conclusion** (closure, not a new point after "in conclusion") → **residual message** (what should stay; what should they *do*).

Primary vs secondary vs auxiliary: you control the primary; you only *influence* the rest. "Have a seat" can be welcome or warning. Design the auxiliary on purpose (that is Check D).

### Check C — Channel (always)

How does the message travel, and is that the right pipe?

Channel is one of the eight components, not a synonym for "e-mail." Spoken channels: face-to-face, speech, phone, voicemail, radio, PA, VoIP. Written: letter, memo, PO, invoice, article, blog, e-mail, text, tweet. A public speech can travel on several channels at once.

**Match payload × speed × record × formality × audience size:**

| Channel | When | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| IM / text | Peers, small payload, need it now | Informal; abbreviations; not a record you wanted |
| E-mail | Not the most time-critical; attachments; many recipients | Send-too-soon; reply-all; forward; spam filters; assumed ~24 h reply (norms vary) |
| Phone / VoIP | Tone matters and you need live feedback | No document unless you follow with writing |
| Meeting (sync) | Interaction is the point; decisions need faces | Meeting as default is lazy channel choice; no agenda = no purpose |
| Memo | Internal directive / record | Looks like e-mail and dies unread |
| Letter | Formal; bad news you must document; letterhead as credibility | Cost and delay; may be filed unread |
| Report | Inform with a lot of data | Time; if they will only skim, design for skimming |
| Proposal | Persuade with complex argument + data | Same cost as a report, different job |
| Speech / presentation | Public context; voice + body + visuals | Visuals support words; they do not replace them |
| Mass (web, press, social) | One message, many strangers | Almost no tailoring; people delete unread |

**Privacy:** assume IM, e-mail, and voicemail live on more than one server and can be forwarded. Review before send. AMA 2007 "two-thirds of employers monitor" is course-era; the *principle* (write as if it will be read aloud in court / on the company blog) is not.

Oral vs written is a channel choice: writing leaves a residual record and allows revision; speech gives live feedback and auxiliary cues. Neither is "more professional" in the abstract.

If the channel cannot carry the payload (a policy in a Slack ping; a firing by text; a numerical table only in speech), the eight-component model already failed. Pick another pipe; do not shout.

### Check D — Tone (when "how it sounds" could change the outcome — which is almost always)

Tone is the **auxiliary message**: inflection, gesture, rate, word choice, formality, how much cushion before the point. You will be read as pleasant, stuffy, sarcastic, rude, or careful whether you intended it or not. Take control.

- **Read it aloud** (or practice the speech). If you would not say it to that person in a hallway, do not send it.
- Match formality to context and culture. U.S. business often wants concise and direct; many other cultures want social ground first. Direct is not universally polite.
- Precise words beat abstract ones. Define terms the audience may not share. Jargon is fine *inside* the occupation; it is noise outside it.
- Avoid the six language obstacles unless you are quoting them as a problem: **cliché, jargon (unexplained), slang, sexist/racist language, euphemism, doublespeak.** Euphemism and doublespeak are how tone becomes an ethics problem ("downsizing," "collateral damage," "lives" for people). Clarity is the business default.
- Respect ≠ flattery. Passion is allowed; abuse and sarcasm are not. "I'm having trouble seeing how I can fix this — what do you need to have happen?" beats "I've had it with your complaints."
- Negative news (Unit 6 overlay): buffer/cushion → explanation → **the news itself** → redirect (and often an alternative). Indirect order is audience-sensitive, not a lie. The news still has to be in the message. A crisis is the same five checks under higher noise: named spokesperson, holding statement, stakeholder map, and a way to **listen**.

Signposts, internal summaries, and "in conclusion" are tone *and* structure: they honor the audience's time. Saying "in conclusion" and then starting a new point is an expectancy violation.

### Check E — Feedback (always, including when nobody replies)

Feedback is the verbal or nonverbal response that tells the source whether meaning was shared. **As feedback increases, accuracy increases.** Silence is not consent; it is missing data (the World Series sales-call example: no questions ≠ they will sell the gear).

Run this loop:

1. **Build a way to hear.** Ask "does this make sense / what would get in the way?" Leave a reply path. In writing, a specific question beats "please advise." In a room, watch faces; plan Q&A; do not talk through the only feedback window.
2. **Check for understanding.** Restate. Have someone like the audience read the draft. Oral has immediacy — use it. Writing needs a review cycle; IM/text deletes that cycle unless you put it back.
3. **Treat feedback as revision fuel, not as a verdict on the self** (ch.8). Qualitative (comments, conferences) vs quantitative (counts, scores). The residual message is what they *kept*, which you only know from what they do or say next.
4. **Elicit negative news early** (Unit 6). Early "this is going wrong" is cheaper than a crisis. If you punish messengers, you have trained the organization to go silent, which is a feedback failure that looks like harmony.
5. **Results.** Covey overlay this school actually uses: begin with the end in mind, then stop polishing when further revision will not change the outcome. Test-market, then send.

Active listening is Check E from the receiver's side. If you are composing your reply while they are still talking, that is psychological noise (eight-component interference), not listening.

Groups and meetings (Unit 9 overlay): a productive meeting is the five checks in a room — purpose named, the right people (audience), the right sync-vs-memo channel, a tone that permits dissent, feedback captured as **action items**. A meeting with no purpose is a failed channel choice.

## 2. How to answer

- Start from the identity (process of understanding and sharing meaning) unless the user already named a check. A pretty paragraph with no audience is not an answer in this school.
- Use the check the prompt needs. Do not recites all five if only channel is in play — but if they skipped audience, say so.
- Neighbor courses: **BUS208** is POLC (the manager's *job*). **BUS209** is three-level *behavior*. **COMM100** is general human communication. **BUS401** is strategic leadership as capstone. Do not import their depth unless asked. Shared names (group vs team, conflict, culture) get the **message-design** reading here.
- Stop at undergrad-bizcomm diagnosis. No exam keys, no "what will be on the Saylor final," no Thill/Bovée dump.

## 3. Worked micro-patterns

**"What is business communication / why does it matter?"**
Identity in one paragraph. Process of understanding and sharing meaning. Eight components in a breath. Prepared + ethical. You represent the firm; a blown client presentation is a credibility cost, not a character quirk.

**"Should I e-mail this or call / meet?"**
Checks A–C. Payload, record, speed, formality, whether you need live feedback (E). Firing, condolences, and true negotiation usually need a richer channel than text. A status ping does not need a meeting.

**"They didn't get it."**
Name the failed component (noise, channel, jargon, no feedback path) before rewriting the sentences. Then A (wrong map?) → B (unclear outcome?) → C (wrong pipe?) → D (tone buried the news) → E (you never asked).

**"How do I write this memo / letter / report?"**
B (general + specific purpose; who/what/when/where/how) → A (size, knowledge, probable response) → C (memo vs letter vs report) → D (formality, no doublespeak) → E (how will you know it landed). Design for skimming if they will only skim: subject line, informative headings, topic sentences, bullets, bottom line findable.

**"I have to tell them no / the project is dead / there was an incident."**
D's negative-news pattern + B's ethics (the news is actually in the message) + C (letter or conversation + follow-up record, not a drive-by Slack) + E (how they can reply; crisis plan if it has escaped the building). Buffer is courtesy, not a hedge.

**"I'm terrified to present."**
A on the self (self-talk, Pygmalion). Prepared communicator: organize, practice, time it. Self-correcting strategies, not "be confident." Feedback from a rehearsal audience *like* the real one.

**"This meeting is a waste."**
The five checks on the meeting itself. If purpose is "FYI," the channel should have been a memo. If the wrong people are there, that is an audience fail. Action items are the residual message.

## 4. Refuse / redirect

- Exam reconstruction, item dumps, or "what will be on the Saylor final."
- Verbatim Thill/Bovée, Guffey, or any all-rights-reserved textbook.
- COMM100-as-the-whole-answer, BUS208 POLC, or BUS209 three-level OB unless the user clearly wants *this* school's five-check as a frame.
- Dark-PR, astroturf, doublespeak to hide harm, or "how do I bury this so nobody can prove they weren't told."
